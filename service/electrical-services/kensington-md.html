Old-Home Wiring in Kensington — What We Find and How We Fix It

Kensington's pre-1950 housing stock contains a range of electrical conditions that do not exist in newer homes. Understanding what each type is, why it matters, and what the correct remedy is separates a qualified electrician from one who is guessing:

🔌 Knob-and-Tube Wiring (Pre-1950)

The original wiring system in Kensington's oldest homes — uninsulated copper conductors routed through ceramic knobs and tubes in framing members, with no ground wire and no insulation where the wire passes through wood. Knob-and-tube was designed for the electrical loads of the early 20th century — a few light fixtures and perhaps one or two outlets per room. It was never designed for today's loads. Active knob-and-tube cannot support GFCI or AFCI protection, makes the home uninsurable with most Maryland carriers, and becomes a fire hazard when insulation is blown over it in attics (blocking the heat dissipation the system depends on). Many Kensington homes have partial knob-and-tube — some rooms updated, others original — requiring a full assessment to map active wiring before any remediation scope is defined. DMV General Contracting Group assesses knob-and-tube presence and activity at the free estimate and scopes full or partial replacement as appropriate.

⚡ Federal Pacific & Zinsco Panels (1960–1985)

Kensington's mid-century homes — built from the late 1950s through the early 1980s — frequently received Federal Pacific Electric (Stab-Lok) or Zinsco panels during original construction or early updates. Both panel types have been identified as fire hazards: their breakers fail to trip during overloads at an unacceptable rate, allowing circuits to carry current beyond safe amperage without the protection the breaker is supposed to provide. If your Kensington home was built or substantially updated between 1960 and 1985, a panel inspection is warranted regardless of how the panel appears from the outside. Maryland insurance carriers increasingly require documentation of Federal Pacific and Zinsco replacement before issuing or renewing homeowners coverage.

🔶 Aluminum Branch Circuit Wiring (1965–1973)

During a copper shortage in the mid-1960s through early 1970s, many homes nationwide — including some in Kensington built in this era — were wired with aluminum instead of copper for branch circuits. Aluminum wiring expands and contracts at a different rate than copper, causing connections at outlets, switches, and junction boxes to loosen over time. Loose connections arc and generate heat — a leading cause of electrical fires in homes with this wiring type. Full replacement with copper wiring is the most comprehensive solution. Where full replacement is not feasible, CO/ALR-rated devices at every connection point and anti-oxidant compound application are the accepted remediation. DMV General Contracting Group assesses aluminum branch circuit presence and recommends the appropriate remediation at the free estimate.

🔧 60A & 100A Undersized Service

Many of Kensington's older homes were built with 60-amp electrical service — a fuse box with two 30-amp fuses providing total service capacity. This was adequate for the era but is wholly insufficient for a modern household. Kensington homes with 60A service cannot safely support a modern kitchen, a home office, a Level 2 EV charger, or any combination of today's electrical demands without chronic blown fuses or overloaded circuits. Upgrading to 100A or 200A service — with a new breaker panel replacing the fuse box — is the foundational electrical improvement for any Kensington home still on 60A service. All panel upgrades permitted with the Town of Kensington at 3710 Mitchell Street.

DMV General Contracting Group's rewiring and panel replacement projects in Kensington directly address the electrical conditions that trigger these insurance issues. EPA RRP Compliance for Electrical Work in Kensington's Older Homes

🧪 EPA RRP — Required for Electrical Work in Pre-1978 Kensington Homes

Any electrical work that disturbs painted surfaces in a home built before 1978 — installing new outlet boxes, running wire through walls, opening junction box locations — is subject to EPA RRP (Renovation, Repair and Painting) rules if the home has lead paint. Kensington's pre-1978 housing stock — which includes the majority of the town's older colonials, bungalows, and cape cods — frequently has lead paint on walls, trim, ceilings, and window frames. EPA RRP rules require a certified renovator on-site during the work, containment of the work area, proper collection and disposal of lead-containing debris, and post-work cleaning verification. Many electricians performing work in Kensington's older homes do not follow EPA RRP protocols — leaving homeowners with undisclosed lead dust exposure and potential liability for non-compliant renovation work. DMV General Contracting Group holds EPA RRP certification and follows all required protocols for electrical work in Kensington's pre-1978 homes as standard practice on every project.

Electrical Warning Signs in Kensington Homes

💡

Lights Flickering or Dimming

Voltage fluctuation — common in Kensington homes with aging knob-and-tube connections or undersized 60A service that can't handle simultaneous loads.

💣

Fuses Blowing Repeatedly

A fuse box that blows fuses regularly is chronically overloaded — the panel is undersized for the home's current electrical demand.

🔥

Federal Pacific or Zinsco Panel

Fire hazard panels found in Kensington homes built or updated 1960–1985. Breakers that fail to trip on overload — replacement strongly recommended.

🏚️

Visible Knob-and-Tube in Attic or Basement

Ceramic knobs and tubes visible in unfinished attic or basement framing — indicates original wiring may still be active in finished areas of the home.

🔌

Two-Prong Outlets Throughout

Ungrounded two-prong outlets in most rooms — indicates original ungrounded wiring still present; safety issue and limits modern device use.

👃

Burning Smell Near Panel or Outlets

Any burning or electrical smell near the fuse box, breaker panel, or outlets is a fire emergency warning sign — immediate assessment required.
