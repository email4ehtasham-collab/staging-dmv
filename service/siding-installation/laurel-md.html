  • Old Town Laurel's Historic District is a genuinely different process: The City of Laurel Historic District Commission (HDC), established in 1975, holds design review authority over seven individual, contiguous historic districts concentrated in the northern part of the city, largely around Main Street. Any exterior siding change on a home inside these boundaries requires a Certificate of Approval from the HDC — reviewing material, profile, and color against the City's published design guidelines — before a building permit application can even be submitted. Since July 2025, residential Certificate of Approval applications carry no filing fee.
  • Fairlawn, West Laurel & Maryland City are Cold War-boom construction: Laurel's population exploded from the mid-1950s through the late 1960s as Fort Meade, the NSA, and the Goddard Space Flight Center drove huge employment growth, and new highways — the Baltimore-Washington Parkway, both Beltways, and I-95 — opened the area to commuters.   • Only part of "Laurel, MD" is actually inside city limits: The City of Laurel's own jurisdiction covers roughly 4.3 square miles along the Patuxent River. Many Laurel-address homes — across zip codes 20707, 20708, 20723, and 20724 — sit outside that boundary in unincorporated Prince George's, Howard, or Anne Arundel County, and permits for those properties go to the relevant county instead of the City's Fire Marshal & Permit Services department. Permitting in Laurel — Two Layers Worth Getting Right

📍 The Laurel permitting reality: Because the City of Laurel's actual jurisdiction covers only about 4.3 square miles, the first question is always whether your property is even inside city limits — many "Laurel, MD" addresses across zip codes 20707, 20708, 20723, and 20724 fall under Prince George's, Howard, or Anne Arundel County instead, and permits go to the county rather than the City's Fire Marshal & Permit Services (FMPS) department. For properties that are inside city limits and also inside one of Laurel's seven Historic Districts, there's a second layer: a Historic District Commission Certificate of Approval is required before FMPS will issue a building permit for exterior siding work, and HDC meetings typically run monthly on the third Tuesday. FMPS permit review itself can take up to 30 business days, longer for complex or Historic District projects. DMV General Contracting Group confirms both your jurisdiction and your Historic District status before filing, so there are no surprises mid-project.
