HPRB & Historic Districts — Interior Flooring Is Never Reviewed

🏛️ What DC's Historic Districts Mean (and Don't Mean) for Flooring

  • Interior flooring is not subject to HPRB review: The Historic Preservation Review Board reviews exterior changes to properties in DC's historic districts and to individually designated DC landmarks. Interior renovations — including flooring of any type — are not reviewed or regulated by HPRB or any DC historic preservation authority. A Capitol Hill or Georgetown homeowner can install LVP, new hardwood, marble tile, or any other flooring material inside their home without any historic preservation review or approval process.
  • When HPRB does apply: If your flooring project is part of a broader renovation that includes exterior scope — a rear addition, a skylight visible from public space, window replacement on a facade, or similar exterior work — that exterior scope requires HPRB review if the property is in a DC historic district or is an individually designated DC landmark. The interior flooring component of the same project is not reviewed; only the exterior components trigger HPRB jurisdiction.
  • The case for period-compatible materials in DC's historic rowhouses: Even though interior flooring is not regulated, the original heart pine, oak, and fir strip hardwood in DC's historic rowhouses is part of what defines the character of Georgetown, Capitol Hill, and Logan Circle as neighborhoods. Refinishing that original flooring preserves the authentic material character of a home that may be 100–140 years old. DMV-GC Group's first recommendation in DC's historic neighborhoods is always to assess whether the original floor is viable for refinishing — not because regulation requires it, but because the original material is genuinely irreplaceable and almost always worth preserving.

Rowhouse Flooring — The DC-Specific Technical Knowledge

DC's historic rowhouses share many flooring characteristics with Garrett Park, Kensington, and Chevy Chase's oldest homes in Maryland. But DC rowhouses have some distinctive conditions worth understanding:

🪵 Heart Pine — DC's Most Distinctive Original Flooring Material: Longleaf heart pine was the dominant flooring material in DC rowhouses built between approximately 1880 and 1930. It is not the same as modern pine — longleaf pine grew slowly over 200+ years, producing an extraordinarily dense, resinous wood that is harder than red oak and has a warm amber-to-honey color that deepens with age. DC rowhouse heart pine floors that have survived under carpet for 50–80 years have not degraded — they have aged. Refinishing reveals a floor with color depth and material density that no modern lumber can replicate. DMV-GC Group identifies heart pine versus oak in DC rowhouses during the consultation, calibrates the sanding approach accordingly (pine is softer than oak and requires lighter drum pressure), and recommends oil-modified polyurethane finishes that complement pine's natural warmth rather than competing with it.
📌 Basement Moisture in DC Rowhouses: DC rowhouses built before 1950 typically have semi-finished or unfinished basements with brick or stone foundation walls that allow seasonal moisture migration into the basement air. Elevated basement humidity affects main-floor hardwood in the same way crawlspace moisture affects Garrett Park bungalows — causing seasonal cupping, gaps, and persistent squeaks. DMV-GC Group assesses basement moisture conditions during the consultation before specifying first-floor hardwood installation in DC rowhouses. Where basement moisture is elevated, engineered hardwood is the more dimensionally stable choice over solid hardwood even above grade, and we recommend it honestly regardless of the homeowner's preference for solid.

For rowhouses: squeak sources identified and addressed from basement access where possible, loose subfloor boards re-secured, soft spots patched. For slab units: concrete moisture tested, self-leveling compound applied where slab has low spots, moisture barrier or moisture-blocking adhesive specified where moisture is elevated.

5

Trim, Transitions & Final Walkthrough
