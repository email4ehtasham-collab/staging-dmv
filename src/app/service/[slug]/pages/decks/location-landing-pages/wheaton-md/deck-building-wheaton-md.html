Concrete Block Foundations in Wheaton — The Detail That Changes Deck Attachment

Wheaton's 1950s–60s split-levels were frequently built with concrete masonry unit (CMU) block foundations — a construction method common in postwar Montgomery County that differs meaningfully from poured concrete and from the wood-frame rim joist attachment used on newer homes. When a contractor who doesn't know this attaches a deck ledger to a CMU wall the same way they would to poured concrete or wood — drilling standard anchor bolts into random block locations — the result is a ledger attached to hollow block cores that can pull out under load. This is the most common structural failure point on Wheaton decks built by contractors unfamiliar with the community's housing stock.

🧱 DMV-GC Group's Concrete Block Ledger Assessment — Wheaton Standard Process

  • Foundation type identification: Before any deck design is finalized for a Wheaton split-level, DMV-GC Group identifies the foundation wall type — poured concrete, CMU block, or wood-frame rim joist — by visual inspection of the visible foundation exterior and, where needed, the basement or crawlspace interior. This is a non-negotiable first step for every Wheaton deck project.
  • CMU block condition assessment: Wheaton's 1950s–60s CMU foundations range from fully sound to partially deteriorated depending on moisture exposure history. We assess visible block condition, mortar joint integrity, and any evidence of water infiltration or spalling before specifying any ledger attachment into the CMU wall.
  • Solid vs hollow block identification: CMU block walls have alternating solid and hollow sections in the core layout. Ledger anchor bolts must bear into solid block material — not hollow core sections that can crack or pull out under the loads a deck applies. DMV-GC Group locates solid block zones using probing before any fastener locations are marked.
  • Correct fastener specification: Where CMU ledger attachment is required, DMV-GC Group specifies heavy-duty masonry anchors or through-bolts of the correct diameter, embedment depth, and spacing for the calculated ledger loads — not generic anchor bolts that meet minimum code on poured concrete but are inadequate for block masonry under higher loads.
  • Ledger flashing: The ledger-to-foundation interface is the primary water infiltration point on any attached deck — poured concrete or CMU. DMV-GC Group installs proper flashing tape and sill seal at every Wheaton ledger connection to prevent moisture migration behind the ledger and into the foundation wall.
  • Post-to-footing alternative: Where CMU wall condition makes ledger attachment inadvisable, DMV-GC Group designs the deck as a freestanding structure — supported entirely on post-and-footing framing without a ledger connection to the house — and connects it to the home with only a joist hanger at the house side without structural load transfer to the wall. This approach eliminates the CMU attachment risk entirely.

Why does a concrete block foundation matter for deck building in Wheaton?
Wheaton's 1950s–60s split-levels were commonly built with concrete block (CMU) foundations. Attaching a deck ledger to CMU requires a different fastener approach than poured concrete — masonry anchors must be placed into solid block zones, not hollow cores, and the block must be structurally sound enough to accept the load. A contractor who doesn't assess the foundation type first risks attaching the ledger improperly, which can result in the deck pulling away from the house. DMV-GC Group identifies foundation type and assesses CMU condition on every Wheaton deck project before any ledger design is specified.
