Why DC Siding Projects Need Local Preservation Expertise

🏛️ The DC Historic Preservation & Rowhouse Case — Explained Honestly

  • Most of DC's older rowhouse fabric sits inside a historic district: With 54 historic districts covering roughly 27,000 buildings, neighborhoods like Capitol Hill, Georgetown, Dupont Circle, Cleveland Park, Kingman Park, and large sections of Petworth are subject to preservation review for exterior work. More than 95 percent of cases are cleared through the HPO's Expedited Review, with same-day approval possible for in-kind repairs — but a full siding replacement using a different material is more likely to need full HPRB review, which meets only twice a month.
  • Front-facade material rules are stricter than most homeowners expect: Documented HPO staff guidance has held that fiber-cement products like James Hardie are not approved for a historic rowhouse's front-facing elevation, even as they're routinely accepted on rear and side walls. Some districts carve out their own exceptions — Kingman Park's design guidelines specifically permit new cementitious siding on frame houses and don't require strict wood-for-wood replacement, reflecting that district's own preservation priorities. Getting the material recommendation wrong on a front elevation means a rejected application and a redesign.
  • Georgetown runs on an entirely separate review process: Georgetown is the one DC historic district where the city's own HPRB is not the primary authority. The US Commission of Fine Arts, through its Old Georgetown Board, reviews exterior work visible from public space — a federal process layered on top of, not instead of, the standard DC Department of Buildings permit. A Georgetown siding project filed as a standard HPRB case is filed with the wrong body entirely.
  • The stakes for skipping review are real: The 2024 Protecting Historic Homes Amendment Act allows the city to fine unpermitted historic-district work up to $25,000 per violation. This isn't a rule DC treats as symbolic — proper sequencing of HPO or HPRB review before a Department of Buildings permit application matters on every historic rowhouse project.

Navigating DC's Historic Preservation Review Process

🏛️ The DC preservation reality: Before recommending a material, we confirm whether a DC property sits within one of the city's 54 historic districts, and if so, which review path applies. For straightforward in-kind repairs, the HPO's Expedited Review can clear a Department of Buildings permit application same-day. Once preservation approval is secured, we file the DC Department of Buildings permit with the approved plans attached.

Frequently Asked Questions — Siding Installation in Washington, DC

Does my Washington DC home need Historic Preservation Review Board approval for new siding?
Very possibly. DC has 54 historic districts covering roughly 27,000 buildings, and exterior work — including siding replacement — on a property in one of them requires historic preservation review before a Department of Buildings permit is issued. More than 95 percent of these cases go through the Historic Preservation Office's Expedited Review, with same-day clearance possible for in-kind repairs using matching materials. Larger changes, such as replacing original wood siding with a different material on a front facade, can require full Historic Preservation Review Board review, which meets twice monthly. Skipping this step is not a minor risk: the 2024 Protecting Historic Homes Amendment Act allows fines up to $25,000 per violation for unpermitted work in a historic district. DMV General Contracting Group checks a property's historic status before recommending any material and handles the HPO or HPRB filing directly.
Can I install James Hardie fiber cement siding on my DC rowhouse's front facade?
It depends on the district and the elevation. DC's Historic Preservation Office has generally not approved fiber-cement products like James Hardie for front-facing elevations in historic districts, based on documented staff guidance, though it is more readily accepted on rear and side elevations not visible from the public right-of-way. Some districts have their own specific rules — Kingman Park's design guidelines, for example, explicitly allow new cementitious siding on frame houses and do not require wood-for-wood replacement. Outside DC's historic districts, James Hardie fiber cement can be installed on any elevation without this restriction. DMV General Contracting Group confirms your property's historic status and district-specific guidelines before recommending a front-facade material, and reserves James Hardie for rear and side elevations or non-historic properties where HPO restrictions don't apply.
Is siding review in Georgetown different from the rest of Washington, DC?
Yes. Georgetown is the one DC historic district where the DC Historic Preservation Review Board is not the primary reviewing authority. Exterior work visible from public space in the Georgetown Historic District, including siding, is reviewed by the US Commission of Fine Arts through its Old Georgetown Board — a federal review process distinct from the HPRB process used everywhere else in the city. A Georgetown siding project still needs a DC Department of Buildings permit, but the design review step runs through this separate federal channel. DMV General Contracting Group prepares Old Georgetown Board submissions specifically, rather than filing them as a standard DC HPRB case.
