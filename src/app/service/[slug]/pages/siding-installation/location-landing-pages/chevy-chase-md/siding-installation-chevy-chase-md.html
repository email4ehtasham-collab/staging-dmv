Why Chevy Chase Siding Projects Need Local Jurisdiction Expertise

🏛️ The Chevy Chase Permitting & Historic Preservation Case — Explained Honestly

  • Chevy Chase is genuinely nine separate local governments: The Town of Chevy Chase (incorporated 1918), Chevy Chase Village (incorporated 1951), the Village of Martin's Additions, Chevy Chase Section 3, Chevy Chase Section 5 (incorporated 1982), the Village of North Chevy Chase, the Village of Drummond — 43 homes on two-thirds of a single street, a special tax district since 1916 — the neighboring Town of Somerset, and an unincorporated area that permits directly through Montgomery County DPS. Several of these villages require their own council or manager sign-off on exterior work in addition to any county permit. A homeowner assuming "Chevy Chase" means one set of rules is usually wrong.
  • Chevy Chase Village adds a full historic-district review layer: Chevy Chase Village is listed on the Montgomery County Master Plan for Historic Preservation. Under County Code Chapter 24A, exterior work that replaces character-defining features — including most full siding replacements — requires a Historic Area Work Permit reviewed by the county's Historic Preservation Commission, guided by the Chevy Chase Village Historic District Guidelines and a Local Advisory Panel appointed through the Village's own Board of Managers. Montgomery County's own historic preservation office specifically flags Chevy Chase Village as a municipality that should review proposals before a HAWP is even filed.
  • A HAWP approval is a first step, not the finish line: Historic Preservation Commission approval of a HAWP does not replace the standard Montgomery County DPS building permit — the approved HAWP plans have to be submitted along with that separate permit application. Skipping either step, or filing them in the wrong order, is a common source of delay on Chevy Chase Village projects.
  • Fiber cement is a real, HPC-approved path for historic homes: County Historic Preservation Commission staff reports show James Hardie-style fiber cement clapboard has been approved as a compatible replacement for deteriorated original wood shingle and clapboard siding in Chevy Chase Village, provided the reveal, profile, and finish are consistent with the district's early-20th-century character — meaning historic-district homeowners are not limited to wood-only replacement.
